Social Security: The Exact Age You Should Claim

This is the highest-stakes math problem in retirement. Claim at 62 and you lock in a permanently smaller check. Wait until 70 and you get the biggest check possible. The difference lasts for life — and it's inflation-adjusted.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

What Each Age Pays

For someone born in 1960 or later (full retirement age 67), here's how claiming age changes a $2,000 full-retirement-age benefit:

Claim Age% of Full BenefitMonthly Check
62 (earliest)70%$1,400
67 (full retirement age)100%$2,000
70 (latest)124%$2,480

Waiting from 62 to 70 boosts your monthly check by 77% — permanently. Delayed retirement credits add 8% per year past full retirement age, and they stop at 70. There's no reason to wait past 70.

The Breakeven Ages

"Breakeven" is the age where the total you've collected by waiting catches up to the total you'd have collected by claiming early:

ComparisonBreakeven Age
Claim 62 vs. 6778 years, 8 months
Claim 62 vs. 7080 years, 4 months
Claim 67 vs. 7082 years, 6 months

The math: claiming at 62 gives you a five-year, $84,000 head start over claiming at 67 (at a $2,000 full benefit). But the age-67 claimer then collects $7,200 more per year. Divide $84,000 by $7,200 and you get 11.67 years — add that to 67 and you land at 78 and 8 months.

Same $2,000 full-retirement-age benefit, no cost-of-living adjustments:

By age 90, the age-70 claimer has collected $124,800 more than the age-62 claimer. And roughly one in three 65-year-olds today will live to 90, per Social Security Administration data.

When Claiming Early Is Right

The breakeven math assumes you'll live long enough to cross it. Claim earlier if:

  • You're in poor health or have a family history of shorter lifespans.
  • You need the income. A smaller check now beats financial stress for a bigger check later.
  • You're still working before full retirement age. The 2026 earnings test withholds $1 for every $2 earned above $24,480 — effectively a temporary cut (withheld benefits are credited back at full retirement age).
  • Your spouse's benefit is much larger. Survivor benefits complicate the math — a surviving spouse generally keeps the larger of the two benefits, which can favor delaying the higher earner's claim.

The Bottom Line

If you're healthy, can afford to wait, and have a normal life expectancy: wait until 70. It's the closest thing to a guaranteed 8%-per-year return that exists — inflation-adjusted, for life. If your health or finances say otherwise, claiming early is a rational choice, not a failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the breakeven age for Social Security?

About 78 years and 8 months when comparing 62 vs. 67; about 82 and 6 months when comparing 67 vs. 70 (for those with a full retirement age of 67).

Is it better to take Social Security at 62 or 67?

If you live past about 79, claiming at 67 pays more in total. If you need income now or expect a shorter lifespan, 62 can be the right call.

Does waiting past 70 increase my benefit?

No. Delayed retirement credits stop at 70 — waiting longer just means fewer checks.

What is the maximum Social Security benefit in 2026?

$2,969/month at 62, $4,152 at full retirement age, and $5,181 at 70, per SSA figures. [verify: confirm current-year maximums at ssa.gov]

Does working affect my Social Security?

Before full retirement age, earning above $24,480 in 2026 triggers benefit withholding ($1 per $2 over the limit). Withheld amounts are credited back at full retirement age.
