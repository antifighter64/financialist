That $5 Coffee Isn't $5. It's $190,000.

The "latte factor" gets mocked as out-of-touch personal finance advice. Just stop buying coffee and you'll be rich! — as if a latte caused the housing crisis.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Here's the thing, though: the math is real even if the messenger is annoying. Small daily expenses, compounded over decades, become life-changing sums. Not because coffee is evil — but because anything you spend $2,000 a year on deserves 30 seconds of thought.

Let's run the actual numbers. All projections assume a 7% average annual return (a conservative long-run figure for a stock-heavy portfolio, well below the S&P 500's ~10% historical average) over 30 years.

Example 1: The Daily Latte — $5.50/day

$5.50 × 365 = $2,007.50/year

Invested instead at 7% for 30 years:

$2,007.50 × 94.46 = $189,630

One daily coffee habit, redirected, is nearly $190,000. That's a paid-off house in many markets, four years of in-state college tuition, or several years of retirement spending.

Example 2: Lunch Out Three Times a Week — $15 × 3

$15 × 3 × 52 = $2,340/year

At 7% for 30 years: $221,052

Nobody's saying pack lunch forever. But the $15 Sweetgreen runs add up to more than the latte does — and most people don't even enjoy them that much by the third week.

Example 3: Subscription Creep — $47/month

Streaming, apps, boxes, memberships you forgot about: the average adds up fast.

$47 × 12 = $564/year

At 7% for 30 years: $53,276

Audit your subscriptions once a year. The ones you'd re-subscribe to today, keep. The rest are a $53,000 donation to companies hoping you never check.

Example 4: Bottled Water — $2/day

$2 × 365 = $730/year

At 7% for 30 years: $68,957

A $30 filter pitcher pays for itself in two weeks. This is the purest latte-factor example there is: identical product, 60x markup, zero lifestyle sacrifice to switch.

Example 5: The "Just $20" Weekend Habit

Takeout, drinks, impulse Amazon orders — call it $20/week you wouldn't miss.

$20 × 52 = $1,040/year

At 7% for 30 years: $98,239

$631,140. That's not "skip coffee and get rich" — that's five utterly ordinary habits quietly eating more than half a million dollars of future wealth.

The Point Isn't Deprivation

The latte factor's critics are right about one thing: cutting a coffee you love to save $5 is a miserable way to build wealth. The real lesson is different:

  1. Automate the redirect. The math only works if the saved money is invested, not absorbed by other spending. Set up an automatic transfer for the amount you cut.
  2. Cut what you don't value. Keep the latte you savor; kill the subscriptions you forgot, the bottled water, the third delivery app fee.
  3. Big wins still matter more. Negotiating a raise, refinancing debt, and choosing the right investments move the needle faster than any coffee math. Do both.

The formula, if you want to run your own numbers:

Future value = Annual spending × [((1.07^30) − 1) ÷ 0.07]

That bracket equals 94.46. So: annual cost × 94.46 = 30-year cost at 7%.

Any daily expense × 365 × 94.46 tells you its true 30-year price in about ten seconds.

FAQ

Q: What is the latte factor?

A: The idea that small, habitual daily expenses (originally a daily latte) compound into large sums over decades when invested instead of spent. A $5.50/day habit is ~$2,000/year — worth ~$190,000 over 30 years at 7% returns.

Q: Is the latte factor actually true?

A: The math is undeniable — it's just compound interest applied to spending. What's debatable is the advice: cutting small joys you love is a poor wealth strategy. The smart version targets spending you don't value.

Q: How much is $5 a day over 30 years?

A: $5/day = $1,825/year. Invested at 7% annual returns for 30 years, that's approximately $172,390.

Q: What's the formula for the latte factor?

A: Multiply the annual cost by 94.46 (the 30-year future-value factor at 7%). So any habit's 30-year cost ≈ yearly spending × 94.46.

Q: Should I stop buying coffee to get rich?

A: No single habit makes you rich. But auditing all your small recurring expenses together — and investing what you cut — can redirect hundreds of thousands of dollars over a career.
