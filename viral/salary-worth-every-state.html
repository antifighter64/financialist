What Your Salary Is Really Worth in Every State

$100,000 in one state is not $100,000 in another. Far from it.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

The Bureau of Economic Analysis — the federal agency that tracks prices nationwide — publishes official "regional price parities" measuring how expensive each state really is. Run $100,000 through their numbers and the gap is staggering: your six-figure salary buys anywhere from $90,334 to $115,075 depending on where you live.

That's a $25,000 swing for the exact same paycheck.

The Biggest Surprise: It's the Housing, Stupid

The BEA data reveals what really drives the gap: rent. California's price level for housing rents is 154.3 — meaning rents run 54% above the national average. West Virginia's is 54.2 — nearly half the national average.

That's a nearly 3-to-1 spread on the single biggest item in most budgets.

This is why a "raise" to move from Columbus to San Francisco can be a pay cut in disguise. A $100K salary in Ohio buys what about $121,000 would need to buy in California. If your new job offers $115K, you're going backward.

The $100K Sweet Spot Strategy

Notice something about the top of the list? Arkansas, Mississippi, Iowa, Oklahoma, the Dakotas. These aren't glamour states — they're purchasing-power states.

A remote worker earning a coastal salary while living in Little Rock or Des Moines gets the best of both worlds: national-pay income at local prices. This arbitrage is one of the biggest legitimate wealth hacks available to ordinary workers.

The Tax Layer Nobody Adds In

The BEA data covers prices — it doesn't include state income tax, which makes the gap even wider. Several states levy no wage income tax at all ([verify]: typically Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, Texas, Washington, Wyoming). A $100K earner in Texas keeps roughly $5,000–$8,000 more per year than the same earner in a high-tax state ([verify] — varies by bracket and filing status).

Stack tax savings on top of price savings and the real gap between the cheapest and priciest states can exceed $30,000 a year — the equivalent of a 30% raise you never had to negotiate.

How to Use This Table

Negotiating a job offer in a new state? Multiply your current salary by the ratio of the two price levels. Moving from Texas (97.5) to Washington (109.8)? You need $100K × (109.8/97.5) ≈ $112,600 just to break even.

Thinking about remote work? Keep the job, move the address. It's the single fastest "raise" most people can get.

Considering a move for retirement? Your savings stretch 20–25% further in the top states — meaning you can retire with a meaningfully smaller number.

FAQ

What is a regional price parity?

The BEA's regional price parity (RPP) is a price index expressed as a percentage of the national average price level (100). A state at 110 is 10% more expensive than average; at 90, 10% cheaper.

Why does housing dominate the rankings?

Housing is the largest household expense, and rent differences between states dwarf differences in groceries, goods, and most services. The BEA publishes rents as a separate sub-index for exactly this reason.

Does this account for quality of life?

No. The table measures purchasing power only — not weather, culture, commute times, or schools. A cheaper state isn't automatically a better one; it just makes your dollar go further.

How current is this data?

The BEA released 2024 RPP data in February 2026. States marked 2022 use the previous release; their relative positions change little year to year.

Is moving to a cheap state always worth it?

Only if your income survives the move. Local wages in low-cost states tend to be lower too. The math works best for remote workers, retirees, and people with portable income.
