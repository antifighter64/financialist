Forget What Influencers Say. Here's the Fed's Data on Rich People's Portfolios.

Every finance guru has a theory about how the rich invest: crypto, art, offshore accounts, secret strategies. The Federal Reserve actually surveyed them — the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), conducted every three years — and the reality is far more boring. And far more copyable.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

The Single Biggest Difference: They Own Stocks. Almost All of Them.

The 2022 SCF asked households whether they own stocks directly or indirectly (including through mutual funds and retirement accounts). The results by income:

Income groupOwn stocks (directly or indirectly)
Bottom 50%34%
50th–90th percentile78%
Top 10%95%

Read that again: 95% of top-decile households own equities. Stock ownership isn't a rich-person quirk — it's nearly universal among high earners, and it's the single clearest behavioral line between wealth tiers. The bottom half mostly doesn't own stocks at all.

Between 2019 and 2022, median family net worth jumped 37% (inflation-adjusted) — the largest three-year gain in the survey's modern history — driven overwhelmingly by rising stock and home values. The people who owned productive assets got richer. The people who didn't, didn't.

What the Wealthy Portfolio Actually Looks Like

The SCF doesn't publish one neat "rich person pie chart," but across its data releases a consistent picture emerges of how high-wealth households allocate:

Asset classTypical wealthy householdTypical middle-class household
Public equities (stocks, funds)Largest share — the growth engineSmall or zero
Private business equitySignificant — SCF data consistently shows business ownership concentrated among high-wealth familiesRare
Real estate (beyond primary home)Common — rental/investment propertyPrimary home is ~most of net worth
Bonds / fixed incomeModerate — stability ballast, grows with ageModest
CashMinimal — emergency buffer only, not a "strategy"Often the largest "investment"

Three patterns stand out:

1. They own productive assets, not savings accounts. Wealthy households keep minimal cash beyond an emergency buffer. Their money works in businesses — public ones (stocks) and private ones (their own companies).

2. Business equity is the stealth wealth-builder. The SCF consistently finds that private business ownership is heavily concentrated among high-wealth households. For many millionaires, the business came first and the portfolio second.

3. Their home is a smaller slice. For middle-class families, home equity is often 60–80% of net worth — one illiquid asset in one zip code. Wealthy households still own homes, but as a smaller fraction of a diversified portfolio.

What They Don't Do

  • They don't day-trade. The data shows buy-and-hold equity ownership through retirement accounts and funds, not speculation.
  • They don't sit in cash "waiting for a crash." Market timing is absent from the wealth-building data; participation is the whole game.
  • They don't chase exotic assets first. Alternatives (private equity, art, crypto) appear at the margins of wealthy portfolios — after the core of stocks, businesses, and real estate is built, not before.

The 3 Habits You Can Copy at Any Income

1. Own equities automatically. You don't need wealth to buy stocks — you need a retirement account and an automatic contribution. The 95%-vs-34% gap isn't about genius; it's about participation. A 401(k) or IRA with a total-market index fund puts you in the 95% club behaviorally.

2. Treat your career like business equity. Most millionaires got there via ownership — a business, or equity in one. If you can't start a company, maximize equity compensation, profit-sharing, or side-business ownership where available.

3. Shrink cash drag. Beyond your emergency fund (3–6 months of essentials), idle cash is a guaranteed loss to inflation. The wealthy keep little of it; neither should you.

FAQ

Q: What percentage of rich people invest in stocks?

A: Per the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, 95% of households in the top income decile own stocks directly or indirectly, versus 78% of the upper-middle and just 34% of the bottom half.

Q: Where do millionaires keep their money?

A: Mostly in productive assets: public equities, private businesses, and real estate. Cash holdings are minimal beyond emergency reserves. Home equity is a smaller share of their net worth than for middle-class households.

Q: Do rich people invest in crypto?

A: Some do at the margins, but Fed survey data shows the core of wealthy portfolios remains stocks, business equity, and real estate. Speculative assets are satellite positions, not the strategy.

Q: How did the wealthy get richer between 2019 and 2022?

A: Median family net worth rose 37% inflation-adjusted — driven by stock market and housing gains. Asset owners captured the gains; non-owners didn't.

Q: Can I invest like the rich with a small income?

A: Behaviorally, yes: automatic equity investing through a retirement account, minimal idle cash, and building ownership (business or equity comp) over time. The data says participation matters more than the starting amount.
