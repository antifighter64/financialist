You're Probably Losing Money on Your Rewards Card

Credit card rewards feel like free money. The bank gives you 2%, 5%, sometimes a shiny "80,000-point bonus," and you feel clever for collecting it.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

But rewards cards are a math problem wearing a marketing costume. Once you subtract the annual fee, account for what the points are actually worth, and compare against a boring flat-rate cash-back card, a lot of "great" cards lose. Here's the net math at three spending levels.

The Baseline: Flat 2% Cash Back, No Annual Fee

This is the card every other card must beat. No categories, no games, no fee:

Annual spendingCash back (2%)Minus feeNet
$24,000$480$0$480
$36,000$720$0$720
$60,000$1,200$0$1,200

Simple, honest, automatic. Remember these numbers — they're the hurdle.

The Challenger: $95-Fee Card With Bonus Categories

A typical mid-tier card: 5% on travel, 3% on dining, 1–2% elsewhere, $95 annual fee. Assume a realistic split — $8,000 in bonus categories (5%), the rest at 2%:

Annual spendingBonus cat. (5%)Rest (2%)Gross−$95 feeNetvs. flat 2%
$24,000$400$320$720$625$625+$145 ✅
$36,000$400$560$960$865$865+$145 ✅
$60,000$400$1,040$1,440$1,345$1,345+$145 ✅

Interesting: with this spend pattern, the fee card wins by exactly $145 at every level. Why constant? Because the fee is fixed and the bonus-category advantage ($8,000 × 3 extra points = $240) minus the $95 fee = $145, regardless of total spend.

But change the pattern and the answer flips. If only $3,000 of your spending hits the 5% category: bonus advantage = $3,000 × 3% = $90, minus $95 fee = −$5. You paid $95 for the privilege of earning less. This is how most people lose: they carry the premium card but spend like a flat-rate customer.

The Break-Even Formula

For any fee card, the math is:

(Extra earn rate × spending in bonus categories) > annual fee

A $95 fee at a 3-percentage-point advantage (5% vs. 2%) breaks even at $3,167/year in bonus-category spending — about $264/month. Below that, the fee card loses. Above it, it wins.

For a $550 premium travel card, the hurdle is brutal: at a 3-point advantage you'd need $18,333/year in bonus categories just to break even on the fee — before counting any perks. Perks (lounge access, travel credits) can close the gap, but only if you'd actually buy those things anyway. A "$200 airline credit" you wouldn't have spent isn't a $200 benefit.

When Points Are a Trap

1. Points devalue. Banks devalue points regularly — what cost 25,000 points last year costs 35,000 this year. Cash back can't be devalued. Hoarding 200,000 points "for someday" is holding a depreciating currency.

2. The 1-point ≠ 1-cent lie. That "5% back" is often 5 points per dollar, and points redeemed for cash are frequently worth 0.6–0.8 cents each. Always convert to cents-per-point before comparing.

3. Rewards change spending. Studies consistently show people spend more when chasing rewards — often 10%+ more. A card that earns you an extra $145 but causes $1,000 in extra spending is a net loss of $855.

4. Interest nukes everything. Carrying a balance at ~20% APR for even a few months wipes out a year of rewards. Rewards cards only work if you pay in full, every month, no exceptions.

The Decision Tree

  • Spend under ~$25K/yr or hate tracking categories? → Flat 2% cash back, no fee. Done.
  • Heavy, consistent bonus-category spender? → Run the break-even formula. If you clear it by 2x, the fee card wins.
  • Frequent traveler who'd buy the perks anyway? → Premium card can win, but audit yearly.
  • Carrying any balance? → Stop. Use a low-APR card, kill the debt, revisit rewards later.

FAQ

Q: Are credit card rewards worth it?

A: Yes — if you pay in full monthly and your bonus-category spending clears the break-even math (roughly $264/month in bonus categories for a typical $95-fee card). Otherwise a flat 2% no-fee card wins.

Q: Is a 2% cash back card better than points?

A: For most people, yes. Cash back has no devaluation risk, no blackout dates, and no redemption puzzles. Points can beat cash for frequent travelers who redeem strategically — but that's a part-time job.

Q: How much do I need to spend to justify an annual fee?

A: Divide the fee by your incremental earn rate. A $95 fee earning 3 extra points per dollar in bonus categories needs ~$3,167/year in those categories to break even.

Q: Do rewards make people spend more?

A: Research consistently finds that rewards incentives increase spending — the psychological reward of "earning points" loosens the purse strings. If your spending rises, the card is costing you money.

Q: Should I hoard points for a big trip?

A: Generally no. Points devalue over time, so earning and burning within 12–18 months beats stockpiling. Treat points like a melting ice cube.
