7 401(k) Myths That Are Secretly Costing You Thousands

Your 401(k) is probably the biggest pile of money you'll ever own. So why do so many of us run it on vibes and half-remembered advice? Let's run the actual numbers.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Myth 1: "I can't afford to contribute right now"

The most expensive myth of all. Every year you wait, you're not losing one year of growth — you're losing the last, most powerful year of compounding.

Take $200 a month invested at an 8% average annual return:

  • Started at 25: roughly $698,000 by age 65
  • Started at 35: roughly $298,000 by age 65
  • The cost of one decade of "not yet": about $400,000

Even small amounts beat nothing by a landslide. And if 200 bucks a month feels steep, remember Vanguard's data: the average employee deferral is just 7.6% of pay.

Myth 2: "I'm enrolled, so I'm saving enough"

Check your default rate. Nearly 4 in 10 auto-enrollment plans still default workers at 3% or below. With the average employer match at 4.7%, a 3% contributor ends up at a combined 7.7% savings rate — barely half of the 12–15% Vanguard recommends.

The fix takes two minutes: bump your contribution to at least 6%, and turn on auto-escalation so it rises 1% a year. Most plans let you set it once and forget it.

Myth 3: "Skipping the employer match is no big deal"

This is the one that makes financial advisors twitch. A typical match is 50 cents on the dollar for the first 6% of your salary. On a $70,000 salary, contributing 6% ($4,200) gets you $2,100 in free money.

That's a 50% instant return — before any investment growth. Skipping it for 30 years at 8% growth costs you roughly $237,000. No investment on Earth hands you a guaranteed 50% return with zero risk.

Myth 4: "I'll just max it out to the $24,500 limit"

For 2026, the IRS employee limit is $24,500 — but only 14% of participants hit the max. If you're under 50, maxing means stashing $2,042 every month, which is out of reach for most people. Don't let the "all or nothing" trap stop you from doing something.

Priorities instead: (1) grab the full employer match, (2) build a 3–6 month emergency fund, (3) then push toward maxing.

And if you're 60–63, you get the SECURE 2.0 "super catch-up": $11,250 extra, for a total of $35,750 in 2026.

Myth 5: "Fees don't matter much"

Fees matter enormously — precisely because they compound too. Compare $100,000 invested for 30 years at 7%:

  • At 0.10% fees: about $753,000
  • At 1.10% fees: about $568,000
  • The fee gap cost you: roughly $185,000

Check your plan's expense ratios. Index funds inside most 401(k)s charge 0.03–0.15%. Actively managed funds often charge 0.50–1.00% or more. The cheaper one almost always wins over time.

Myth 6: "Cashing out when I change jobs is fine"

Every year, millions of people cash out small 401(k)s at job changes. A $15,000 cash-out at 25 doesn't cost $15,000 — it costs what $15,000 would become: about $326,000 by age 65 at 8% growth.

Plus you pay income tax plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty right now. Roll it over instead. It takes one phone call.

Myth 7: "My plan doesn't offer much, so why bother"

Even a bare-bones plan with no match beats no plan. Contributions cut your taxable income dollar for dollar (pre-tax option), grow tax-deferred, and the 2026 combined employee-plus-employer ceiling is $72,000.

The one-line takeaway: contribute at least enough to grab the full match, nudge your rate up 1% a year, and pick the cheapest index funds in your plan. That's 90% of 401(k) success right there.

FAQ

Q: What's the 401(k) contribution limit in 2026?

A: $24,500 for employees under 50. Workers 50+ can add $8,000 ($32,500 total), and workers 60–63 get a super catch-up of $11,250 ($35,750 total), per IRS Notice 2025-67.

Q: What happens if I contribute too much?

A: Excess deferrals get taxed twice — once when contributed, once when withdrawn. Your plan will return the excess if you catch it before April 15 of the following year.

Q: Should I choose Roth or traditional 401(k)?

A: If you expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement, traditional saves you taxes now. If you expect higher income later, Roth locks in today's rate. Many savers split between both.

Q: Is borrowing from my 401(k) really that bad?

A: You miss market growth while the money's out, repayments are made with after-tax dollars, and if you leave your job you may have to repay the balance immediately or face taxes and penalties.

Q: What should my savings rate be?

A: Vanguard recommends a combined 12–15% of pay, including the employer match. Fidelity's research lands on 15% starting at age 25.
