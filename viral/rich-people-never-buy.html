9 Things Rich People Never Waste Money On

Warren Buffett has lived in the same Omaha house since 1958. One of the world's richest men eats McDonald's for breakfast and has said he won't pay more than a set amount for a haircut. ([verify] specific anecdotes — widely reported in interviews)

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

These aren't quirks. They're a philosophy: the wealthy get rich by refusing to spend on things that don't matter, so they can spend massively on things that do.

Here are the nine spending categories rich people consistently avoid.

1. Brand-New Cars

Cars are the fastest way to set money on fire. A new car loses roughly 20% of its value in the first year and ~15% per year after that ([verify] depreciation figures). Wealthy buyers overwhelmingly buy 2–3-year-old cars — same vehicle, 40% off — or keep one car for a decade. The car you drive is the most visible wealth signal, and the rich know visibility costs money.

2. Credit Card Interest

This one is nearly universal among the wealthy: they use credit cards for points and pay the balance in full. Carrying a balance at 20%+ APR is mathematically equivalent to lighting a fifth of your spending on fire. The rich borrow strategically (low-rate leverage on appreciating assets) and never pay interest on depreciating consumption.

3. Lifestyle Creep

Ask most millionaires what they'd do with a raise and the honest ones say: save it. Studies of millionaire households consistently find that the #1 trait isn't income — it's living below their means even as income rises ([verify] — echoes "The Millionaire Next Door" findings). The raise goes to investments, not the house, the car, or the vacation tier.

4. "As Seen on TV" Upgrades

Designer labels, the newest phone every year, premium versions of things that work fine in basic form — the wealthy are ruthless about the difference between quality and branding. They'll spend $5,000 on a mattress (they use it 8 hours a day) and wear a $20 t-shirt. The rule: spend on what you touch daily, cheap out on what impresses strangers.

5. Lottery Tickets and Gambling

Expected value of a Powerball ticket: deeply negative. Odds of the jackpot are 1 in 292.2 million. Wealthy people understand expected value intuitively — they don't buy negative-expectation products as a "strategy." (A $2 ticket for fun is entertainment; a $50/week habit is a tax on people who can't do math.)

6. Extended Warranties and "Protection Plans"

Retailers push these because the margins are enormous — often 50%+ profit ([verify]). The wealthy self-insure small risks. If you can afford to replace the appliance, the warranty is just an overpriced lottery ticket in reverse.

7. Keeping Up With Other Rich People

This is the silent killer. New wealth tries to look rich; old wealth tries to stay rich. The people with real money are the ones not performing wealth — which is exactly why you can't identify them by their clothes, cars, or Instagram.

8. Fees They Can Avoid

Late fees. Overdraft fees. ATM fees. High-fee mutual funds. The wealthy automate bills (never late), keep fee-free accounts, and obsess over expense ratios — because a 1% annual fee on investments compounds into hundreds of thousands over a lifetime.

9. Their Own Time (Spent Cheaply)

Here's the flip side: rich people are stingy about money and generous about buying back time. They'll drive a 10-year-old car but pay for housecleaning, grocery delivery, and tax professionals. The logic is cold: an hour of their time deployed well is worth more than the cost of outsourcing the chore. Being "frugal" with time is the actual flex.

The Pattern

Every item on this list shares one trait: the spending has no return. Rich people aren't cheap — they're allergic to spending that doesn't either appreciate, produce income, or buy back meaningful time and health.

The question isn't "what would a rich person buy?" It's "what would a rich person refuse to buy?" — and the answer is: anything that makes them look rich while making them poorer.

FAQ

Are all rich people frugal?

No — plenty spend lavishly. But research on self-made millionaires (notably "The Millionaire Next Door") consistently finds frugality and living below one's means among the most common traits.

Does being cheap actually make you rich?

Frugality alone doesn't create wealth — it creates surplus, which invested over time creates wealth. The saving is the engine; investing is the fuel.

What's the single biggest waste of money?

For most households: car payments (depreciation + interest on a depreciating asset) and credit card interest. Together they can exceed $10,000/year for an average family.

Do rich people really drive old cars?

Many do. Surveys of millionaires repeatedly show the most popular cars are mainstream brands (Toyota, Honda, Ford), not luxury marques ([verify] — consistent with Millionaire Next Door data).

Isn't this just "live like you're poor" advice?

No — it's "spend where it counts" advice. The wealthy spend freely on health, education, experiences, and time. They cut spending that exists only to signal status.
