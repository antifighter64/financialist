How Long $1 Million Lasts in Every State

Same million dollars. Wildly different retirements.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Our $1M test: take the Bureau of Labor Statistics' average annual household spending — $78,535 (2024) — adjust it by each state's cost-of-living index, and divide $1 million by the result. Retirees typically spend less than average households, so treat these as conservative floors.

Hawaii is in a league of its own: $1 million buys less than 7 years there — under half what it buys in Oklahoma. Housing is the reason: Hawaii's housing costs run roughly 4.5x Oklahoma's, and housing is the largest share of retiree spending.

What this table is really saying

Housing is the whole game. Grocery prices barely differ across states — the gap between Oklahoma and Hawaii is almost entirely housing, which runs ~4.5x higher in Hawaii and is the largest share of retiree spending.

Taxes and Social Security aren't in this table. These figures exclude state taxes (a retiree in income-tax-free Tennessee keeps more per withdrawal than one in California) and Social Security — the average $24,852/year benefit stretches every figure here by roughly a third.

The move that beats saving more

The gap between #1 and #50 is 7.9 years of retirement. To buy that through saving alone at $500/month and 8% returns, you'd need to start roughly a decade earlier. Or you could just move.

FAQ

Q: In which state does $1 million last the longest?

A: Oklahoma — about 14.8 years at average spending levels, thanks to the nation's lowest cost-of-living index (86.0). Mississippi and West Virginia round out the top three.

Q: Does this include Social Security?

A: No — and that's conservative. Adding the average $24,852/year Social Security benefit extends every figure substantially. Think of this table as the "savings alone" baseline.

Q: Does investment growth change the math?

A: Yes, favorably. This table assumes 0% growth; a conservatively invested portfolio earning 4–5% could add 20–30% more years. Inflation works the other way — over long periods the two roughly wrestle to a draw.

Q: Should I move states just to stretch my savings?

A: It's the highest-leverage move in retirement planning — but weigh healthcare access, taxes on your income mix, moving costs, and whether you'll be happy there. Cheap and miserable is a bad trade.
