Everything You "Know" About Credit Scores Is Costing You Money

Credit scores control your mortgage rate, your car loan, your apartment application, sometimes even your job offer. And most of what people believe about them is wrong — often exactly backwards.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Here's the ground truth first. FICO publishes exactly how its score (300–850) is weighted:

FactorWeightWhat it means
Payment history35%Do you pay on time, every time
Amounts owed30%How much of your available credit you're using
Length of credit history15%Age of your accounts
New credit10%Recent inquiries and new accounts
Credit mix10%Variety of account types

Payment history plus amounts owed = 65% of your score. Everything else is details. Now let's kill the myths.

Myth 1: "Checking my score hurts it"

Fact: Checking your own score is a soft inquiry — zero impact, always. What's mildly damaging is a hard inquiry, when a lender pulls your credit for an application (about 10% of your score, and only a few points for a year or so). The people who check their scores most often tend to have the highest scores, because monitoring catches errors and fraud early. Check freely.

Myth 2: "Carrying a balance builds credit"

Fact: This is the most expensive myth on the list. Carrying a balance does nothing for your score — FICO doesn't reward you for paying interest. What matters is utilization: the percentage of your credit limit reported as used. You get the identical score benefit by charging $500 and paying it in full as by charging $500 and revolving it — except the second version costs you ~20% APR. Pay in full. Every month.

Myth 3: "Closing old cards helps my score"

Fact: Usually the opposite. Closing your oldest card hurts you twice: it shortens your length of credit history (15% of your score) and it reduces your total available credit, which raises your utilization ratio (30% of your score). Example: $2,000 balance on $10,000 total limits = 20% utilization. Close a $5,000-limit old card and it's suddenly $2,000 on $5,000 = 40% utilization — score drops, for no benefit. Keep old cards open; use them once a year so the issuer doesn't close them.

Myth 4: "My income and savings affect my score"

Fact: FICO scores contain zero information about your income, savings, employment, or net worth. A broke person with perfect payment habits outscores a millionaire who pays late. (Lenders consider income separately when you apply — but the score itself is purely about credit behavior.)

Myth 5: "One late payment ruins everything forever"

Fact: A single 30-day late payment hurts — it can drop a high score significantly — but it's not permanent. FICO weighs recency, frequency, and severity: recent lates hurt more than old ones, and the damage fades with every subsequent on-time payment. One slip five years ago amid years of clean history is a footnote, not a life sentence. The fix is boring: never miss twice, and set autopay for at least the minimum on everything.

Myth 6: "I need to stay under 30% utilization"

Fact: Under 30% is fine, but it's a ceiling, not a target. Lower is better — the highest scorers typically use under 10%. And here's the trick most people miss: utilization has no memory in most FICO versions. It's a snapshot of your last reported balances. So you can run 40% utilization all year, pay down to 5% the month before a mortgage application, and your score reflects the 5%. Time big applications accordingly.

Myth 7: "All debt is equally bad for my score"

Fact: FICO's "amounts owed" (30%) cares mostly about revolving utilization — credit cards. Installment debt (mortgage, auto, student loans) is scored more gently; owing $200,000 on a mortgage bothers FICO far less than maxing out a $10,000 credit card. This is why someone with a mortgage and car loan can have a higher score than someone debt-free but with maxed cards. (Being debt-free is still great — it just doesn't maximize the score.)

The 5 Moves That Actually Raise Your Score

Ranked by impact, mapped to the weights:

  1. Autopay the minimum on everything (35% — payment history). One system beats willpower.
  2. Get utilization under 10% before any application (30% — amounts owed). Pay down, or request limit increases you don't use.
  3. Never close your oldest card (15% — history length). Downgrade fee cards instead of closing.
  4. Space out applications (10% — new credit). Batch rate-shopping within 14–45 days so multiple mortgage/auto inquiries count as one.
  5. Dispute errors. You're entitled to free reports; wrong collections and mistaken accounts are depressingly common and very fixable.

FAQ

Q: What are the 5 factors of a FICO credit score?

A: Payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%), and credit mix (10%). Scores range from 300 to 850.

Q: Does checking my credit score lower it?

A: No. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry with zero impact. Only hard inquiries from lender applications affect your score, and only slightly.

Q: Does carrying a credit card balance help my credit score?

A: No — this is a costly myth. Paying in full builds the exact same history without the ~20% interest. Only your reported utilization matters, not whether you revolve.

Q: Should I close old credit cards I don't use?

A: Generally no. Closing old cards shortens your credit history and raises your utilization ratio — both lower your score. Keep them open with occasional small charges.

Q: How fast can I raise my credit score?

A: Utilization improvements can show up in 30–60 days (one billing cycle). Late-payment damage fades over months to years of clean history. There's no legitimate overnight fix — anyone selling one is selling a scam.
