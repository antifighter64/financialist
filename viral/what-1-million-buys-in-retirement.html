What $1 Million Actually Buys You in Retirement

A million dollars. The most famous number in personal finance — and the most misunderstood.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Northwestern Mutual found Americans now think they need $1.46 million to retire comfortably, while the average person has saved just $88,400. But what does the magic million itself actually buy? Less than the movies suggest — and more than the pessimists claim.

Start with the 4% rule

The classic guideline: withdraw 4% in year one, adjust for inflation after. On $1 million, that's $40,000 a year — designed to last 30 years.

Add the average Social Security check — $2,071/month in 2026, or about $24,852/year — and a typical retiree with $1M saved has roughly $64,852/year of income. That's the real budget. Not bad. Not yachts.

Three lifestyles $1 million buys

The Essentials life — ~$50,000/year → 20 years

Paid-off modest home, one car, cooking at home, local life. Totally doable in low-cost states. Boring? Maybe. Secure? Absolutely.

The Comfortable life — ~$75,000/year → about 13 years

Nice home, regular travel, dining out, hobbies, gifts for grandkids. This is what most people picture as "retirement." The math says $1M alone funds about 13 years of it — Social Security stretches that considerably further.

The Premium life — ~$110,000/year → about 9 years

Second home or luxury condo, international travel, country club. $1M funds less than a decade of this. The premium life needs $2M+ or serious ongoing income.

Years assume no investment growth and no Social Security; both extend every scenario.

Your state changes everything

$1 million is not the same everywhere. Adjusting for the Bureau of Economic Analysis's 2024 regional price parities:

The uncomfortable truth about the 4%

The 4% rule was built for a 30-year retirement with a specific stock/bond mix. Three things break it:

  1. Retiring into a crash — withdrawing 4% while the market drops 20% does permanent damage (sequence-of-returns risk)
  2. Living past 95 — the rule targets 30 years; a 65-year-old couple has decent odds one partner hits 90+
  3. Healthcare — the average 65-year-old couple faces hundreds of thousands in lifetime medical costs; the 4% rule never budgeted for that

The fix isn't a different number — it's flexibility. In down years, spend less. In good years, spend a bit more. Dynamic beats rigid.

So is $1 million enough?

For a paid-off homeowner in a low-cost state with Social Security: yes, comfortably. For a renter in California with champagne tastes: not even close.

The honest formula: (Your annual spending − Social Security − pensions) × 25 = your number. If you spend $80,000 and get $25,000 from Social Security, your number is $1.375 million. If you spend $55,000, it's $750,000.

$1 million isn't a finish line. It's a down payment on the math.

FAQ

Q: Is $1 million enough to retire at 65?

A: With the 4% rule it generates $40,000/year plus ~$24,852 in average Social Security — about $65,000 total. Enough in low-cost states with a paid-off home; tight in expensive coastal cities.

Q: What is the 4% rule?

A: A retirement guideline from 1994 research: withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust for inflation annually. Designed to make savings last 30 years.

Q: How much do I need to retire if I spend $100,000 a year?

A: Using the 25x rule: ($100,000 − expected Social Security) × 25. With $25,000 in Social Security, that's $1.875 million.

Q: Does $1 million include my house?

A: It shouldn't, for income math. Home equity doesn't pay grocery bills unless you sell, downsize, or tap a reverse mortgage. Count investable assets separately.

Q: Why do people say you need $1.46 million now?

A: That's Northwestern Mutual's 2024 "magic number" — what Americans think they need, up from $951,000 in 2019. Expectations rose faster than savings: the average person has saved only $88,400.
