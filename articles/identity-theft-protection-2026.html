1. OmniWatch — Best Overall Protection

OmniWatch is the newest serious player (launched late 2025) and the most complete: three-bureau credit monitoring, dark web surveillance, identity theft insurance, and full-service restoration — meaning a real human does the cleanup work if your identity is stolen.

What you get:

  • Monitoring across all three credit bureaus
  • Dark web and data broker scanning
  • Identity theft insurance policy included
  • White-glove restoration: they handle the calls, paperwork, and disputes

What it costs: Paid plans only — but this is a case where paid beats free. Free monitoring tells you after the damage. OmniWatch's insurance and restoration team fixes it.

The catch: It's a newer brand. The feature set is strong on paper, and early reviews are positive, but it doesn't have the decade-long track record of legacy players.

Verdict: If you want one service that covers everything and actually helps you recover, this is it.

2. Experian — Best Free Option

Experian's free tier gives you what most people actually need: your Experian credit report, FICO score monitoring, and dark web surveillance for your email. It's genuinely free, no credit card required.

What you get free:

  • Experian credit report and FICO score
  • Credit monitoring alerts
  • Dark web scan for your personal info

What you get paid: Three-bureau monitoring, more frequent score updates, and additional identity features.

The catch (and it's a big one): Experian's business model is lead generation. The free tools exist to sell you credit cards, loans, and insurance — they earn up to $50 every time you open a product through their marketplace. Your "free" monitoring is funded by marketing to you. That's the trade. Read our research piece on the hidden cost of free credit monitoring for the full breakdown.

Verdict: Best free monitoring available. Just know what you're trading for it.

4. DeleteMe — Best for Removing Your Data

Different approach: instead of monitoring for theft, DeleteMe removes your personal information from data broker sites — the companies that sell your name, address, phone number, and relatives to anyone who pays.

What Identity Theft Protection Should Actually Include

Not all "protection" is equal. Here's what matters, in order:

  1. Three-bureau credit monitoring. Thieves don't limit themselves to one bureau. Single-bureau monitoring misses two-thirds of new-account fraud.
  2. Dark web surveillance. Your Social Security number is probably already for sale. Knowing when it surfaces lets you freeze credit before it's used.
  3. Identity theft insurance. Covers the out-of-pocket costs of recovery — legal fees, lost wages, notary costs. Usually $25K–$1M in coverage.
  4. Full-service restoration. The difference between a 100-hour nightmare and a phone call. This is the feature that matters most when things go wrong.
  5. Data broker removal. Shrinks the pool of personal info available to thieves in the first place.

The Free vs. Paid Truth

Free monitoring (Experian, WalletHub, Credit Karma) is real and useful. But understand the economics: these companies make $12–$50 every time you sign up for a financial product through them. You're the product being sold to lenders.

Paid protection (OmniWatch, DeleteMe) makes money from you directly, which means their incentive is keeping you safe — not selling you a credit card.

Our recommendation: use both. Free monitoring for daily awareness, paid protection for actual defense. Total cost: less than $20/month for coverage that could save you 100+ hours and thousands of dollars.

How Identity Theft Actually Happens in 2026

Forget the movie version (someone digging through your trash). Here's how it really works now:

Data breaches. Billions of records leak every year — Social Security numbers, addresses, dates of birth. Your info is in breach databases right now. Check haveibeenpwned.com if you don't believe it.

SIM swapping. Thieves convince your phone carrier to move your number to their phone. Then they intercept your two-factor codes and drain your accounts. Call your carrier and set up a port-out PIN today.

Tax fraud. Someone files a return with your SSN before you do and collects your refund. The IRS is slow to catch it. Filing early is your best defense.

Synthetic identity fraud. Thieves combine a real SSN (often a child's) with a fake name to build a whole new identity. It's the fastest-growing type because it's hardest to detect — the victim doesn't know for years.

AI voice scams. Thieves clone a family member's voice from social media audio and call grandparents asking for emergency money. It's terrifying and it's real.

No single service stops all of these. That's why layering matters: freeze your credit, use monitoring, remove your data from brokers, and enable every security feature your bank offers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need identity theft protection?

If you have a Social Security number, a bank account, or file taxes — yes. Over 1 in 4 Americans has experienced some form of identity fraud. The question isn't whether your data is exposed (it is), but whether you'll catch misuse early.

Can't I just freeze my credit for free?

Yes, and you should — it's free at all three bureaus and it's the single best anti-fraud step. But freezes don't stop tax fraud, medical identity theft, or misuse of your existing accounts. Protection services cover what freezes can't.

What should I do first if my identity is stolen?

  1. File a report at IdentityTheft.gov (FTC)
  2. File a police report
  3. Freeze your credit at all three bureaus
  4. Contact the fraud departments of affected companies
  5. If you have a restoration service, call them — they'll handle steps 1–4 for you

Is identity theft insurance worth it?

The insurance itself is cheap (often bundled). The real value is the restoration service that comes with it. Paying $150/year to avoid a 100-hour cleanup is one of the best deals in personal finance.

Best privacy play: DeleteMe — get your data off broker sites.

Layer them. Free monitoring for awareness, paid protection for defense.
