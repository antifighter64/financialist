The Tax Software Money Machine: What "Free Filing" Really Costs

Every January, every finance website publishes the same article: "Best Tax Software 2026." Every one of them ranks TurboTax, TaxAct, and a few others. None of them tell you what they earn when you click.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Every January, every finance website publishes the same article: "Best Tax Software 2026." Every one of them ranks TurboTax, TaxAct, and a few others. None of them tell you what they earn when you click.

We will. Here's the affiliate money behind tax season — and what "free filing" actually costs you.

Read that again: LibertyTax pays affiliates 35% of every sale. More than a third of what you pay goes to the website that referred you.

The 35% Question

Why does LibertyTax pay 35% while TurboTax pays 15%?

Market position. TurboTax dominates — roughly 40%+ of the consumer tax software market (we estimate, based on Intuit's reported filer numbers vs. IRS totals). They don't need affiliates as desperately. Their 15% is a volume play: millions of referrals × 15% × $80 average sale.

LibertyTax is the challenger. They pay 35% because they need the distribution. Every affiliate dollar buys shelf space on "best of" lists that TurboTax owns by default.

What this means for rankings: a site that ranks LibertyTax #1 and TurboTax #3 isn't necessarily wrong — but they're earning 2.3x more from the #1 pick. You'll never see that disclosed in the article.

The Cookie Wars

Look at the cookie lengths: E-file.com gives 120 days. TurboTax gives 7 days (web referral).

The cookie is the attribution window — how long after your click the site gets credit for your purchase. A 120-day cookie means: click in January, buy in April, the site still gets paid. A 7-day cookie means: click now, buy now, or the credit evaporates.

Why TurboTax can afford a 7-day cookie: brand dominance. You'll buy TurboTax anyway; the affiliate just needs to be the last click. Short cookie, still massive volume.

Why E-file.com offers 120 days: they're buying patience. They know you'll comparison-shop, and they want the affiliate to keep the credit through your entire decision.

The "Free Filing" Funnel

Here's the business model behind every "free" tax product:

Step 1: Advertise free. "Free federal filing!" gets you in the door. The IRS Free File program technically covers ~70% of taxpayers, but only a small fraction use it (we estimate low single digits based on IRS participation reports).

Step 2: Upsell the state return. Federal free, state $30–$45. This is where most "free" filers start paying.

Step 3: Upsell the complexity. Got investments? Rental income? Self-employment? That's Deluxe/Premium/Self-Employed tier: $60–$120.

Step 4: Upsell the extras. Audit defense ($45). Live expert help ($50+). Refund advance (free, but locks you in).

The average "free" filer who actually files free: a minority. Industry analyses have repeatedly found that most users who start on free tiers upgrade at least once during the process.

The affiliate angle: the site earns 10–35% of whatever you end up paying — including every upsell. A $0 free filer earns the site $0. A $120 Deluxe + state filer earns the site $12–$42. Guess which outcome the "best tax software" article is optimized for?

The January–April Content Gold Rush

Tax content is the most seasonal play in finance publishing:

  • January: "Best tax software" articles go live. Search volume starts climbing.
  • February–March: peak. "TurboTax vs TaxAct" comparisons spike. Every click is worth potential 10–35% commissions.
  • April 15: cliff. Volume dies overnight.
  • October: extension deadline brings a mini-surge.

Publishers spend 3 months earning a year's worth of tax affiliate revenue in 90 days. That's why the articles are so aggressive, so long, and so uniformly positive — there's no time for nuance when the window is this short.

What You Actually Need

Cut through the affiliate noise:

Simple return (W-2 only, standard deduction): file genuinely free. The IRS Free File program or FreeTaxUSA's free tier handles this. Don't let anyone upsell you.

Moderate complexity (investments, HSA, itemizing): you need a paid tier, but compare: TaxAct at 10% commission incentive vs. TurboTax at 15% — the site prefers TurboTax, but TaxAct is often $20–$30 cheaper for equivalent features. The cheaper product with the lower affiliate payout is frequently the better deal for you. Funny how that works.

Self-employed / rental / complex: this is where paid software earns its keep. But consider: a local CPA charges $200–$400 and finds deductions software misses. If your situation is genuinely complex, human beats algorithm.

The Disclosure Test

Next January, read any "best tax software" article and ask:

  1. Does it disclose the affiliate commission rate? (No.)
  2. Does the #1 pick have the highest commission? (Check: LibertyTax 35% vs. TurboTax 15%. If LibertyTax is #1 everywhere, you know why.)
  3. Does it mention IRS Free File? (Often buried or absent — there's no commission in free.)

We participate in these programs too. The difference: you now know the rates.

Bottom Line

35%. 30%. 15%. 10%. That's what tax software companies pay for your click — and it shapes every ranking you read from January to April.

File free if you can. Compare on price if you can't. And remember: the site that recommended your $120 tax package just earned $18–$42 from your purchase. That doesn't make the recommendation wrong. It makes you informed.
