The Hidden Cost of Free Credit Monitoring: What Experian and WalletHub Make Per Signup

Free credit monitoring is one of the best deals in personal finance. It's also one of the most misunderstood.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Free credit monitoring is one of the best deals in personal finance. It's also one of the most misunderstood.

Experian gives you a free FICO score. WalletHub gives you daily credit updates. Credit Karma gives you two free scores. All free. No catch — except the catch is you.

Here's exactly how the "free" credit monitoring economy works, what these companies earn from your signup, and what it costs you.

What They Make Per Signup

From live affiliate program terms (October 2026), here's what Experian and WalletHub pay affiliates — which tells you what a signup is worth to them:

Experian pays:

  • $50 per Smart Money checking account opened
  • $15 per subscription cancellation signup (yes, they pay for this)
  • $12 per CreditMatch/Personal Loan Match free account signup
  • $12 per auto insurance lead form
  • $10 per Smart Money free registration

WalletHub pays:

  • $50 per Premium/Premium+ subscription lead (free trial counts)

What this tells us: A free Experian account signup is worth at least $12 to Experian (that's what they'll pay someone else to get you). A free trial signup for WalletHub Premium is worth $50. These aren't guesses — they're contracted payouts.

The Business Model, Step by Step

Step 1: Give away the score

Your credit score costs Experian almost nothing to show you. They already have your data — they're one of three companies legally entitled to collect it. Displaying it in an app is a marginal cost close to zero.

But that free score gets you in the door. You're now a registered user with a verified identity, known credit range, and demonstrated financial intent.

Step 2: Learn everything about you

Every interaction teaches them:

  • You checked your score 14 times this month → you're anxious about credit (prime target for credit-building products)
  • Your score jumped 40 points → you might qualify for better cards (time to show premium offers)
  • You have 3 hard inquiries → you're shopping for credit right now (highest-value moment)
  • You opened a CreditMatch account → you've told them your income, housing, and goals

This isn't hypothetical. It's the product.

Step 3: Sell access to you

Here's where the money is made. Experian's marketplace shows you "matched" credit cards, loans, and insurance products. Every time you apply through those links, Experian earns:

  • $150+ per approved credit card (industry standard)
  • $25–$225 per loan application (we documented LendingTree at $25, BMO at $225)
  • $12–$75 per insurance lead (Experian's own terms show $12; New York Life pays $75)

One user who opens a credit card through Experian's marketplace is worth more than 10 users who just check their score.

Step 4: Upsell the subscription

The free tier is the funnel. The paid tier is the margin.

  • Experian Premium: ~$25/month for three-bureau monitoring
  • WalletHub Premium: ~$10/month for enhanced features

They only need 4–5% of free users to convert to paid. At $25/month, 1,000 paying users = $300,000/year in recurring revenue from a product that costs almost nothing to operate.

That's a 5–8x return on free. "Free" is the most profitable word in fintech.

What It Costs You

Let's be fair: the monetary cost to you is $0. But there are real costs:

1. Your attention is the product

Every "You're pre-approved!" notification is calibrated to get you to apply. The average American receives 3–5 credit offers per week through these apps. Most are for products you don't need.

2. Unnecessary hard inquiries

Every application you submit through their marketplace triggers a hard inquiry. Too many in a short period dings your score — the very score you're there to monitor.

3. The illusion of action

Checking your score feels productive. It's not. Your score only changes when your financial behavior changes — paying down balances, not opening new cards. The app's real goal is keeping you engaged enough to eventually click an offer.

4. Data exposure

You've given a complete financial profile to a company whose revenue depends on selling access to that profile. Their privacy policies allow "sharing with partners" — which is the entire business model.

How to Use Free Monitoring Without Getting Used

We're not telling you to delete these apps. Free monitoring is genuinely useful. Just use it on your terms:

Do:

  • Check your score monthly (not daily — it doesn't change that fast)
  • Set up fraud alerts (the actual valuable feature)
  • Use it to catch errors on your report
  • Dispute inaccuracies (this is free and your legal right)

Don't:

  • Apply for products through their marketplace without comparing elsewhere first
  • Upgrade to paid unless you specifically need three-bureau monitoring
  • Treat "pre-approved" as a recommendation — it's an advertisement
  • Give them more data than needed (skip the optional income/lifestyle surveys)

The 5-minute privacy cleanup:

  1. Open app settings → Privacy
  2. Opt out of "marketing communications" and "partner offers"
  3. Disable push notifications for offers (keep fraud alerts on)
  4. Don't link external bank accounts unless you need the feature

The Paid Alternative

If the trade bothers you, paid identity protection flips the model: you pay them, so they work for you.

  • OmniWatch — full monitoring, insurance, and restoration. Their revenue comes from your subscription, not from selling you credit cards.
  • DeleteMe — removes your data from broker sites. No marketplace, no upsell funnel.

Neither is free. That's the point. When you're the customer instead of the product, incentives align.

The Uncomfortable Truth

Experian is a $20 billion company. They didn't get there selling $25/month subscriptions.

They got there because 100+ million Americans gave them their financial lives for a free number — and Experian sells access to those lives for billions.

Use the free score. Just know what you're paying with.
