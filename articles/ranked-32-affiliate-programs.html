We Ranked 32 Affiliate Programs by Pay-Per-Application: The Real Numbers

Nobody publishes this. Affiliate networks show you payouts, but nobody lines them all up and ranks them by what actually matters: how much you get paid per user action.

By Financialist · October 17, 2026

Nobody publishes this. Affiliate networks show you payouts, but nobody lines them all up and ranks them by what actually matters: how much you get paid per user action.

We did. We pulled the live program terms for 32 financial and insurance affiliate programs and ranked every one by dollar-per-application, dollar-per-lead, or dollar-per-signup — not per closed deal, not per funded loan. Just the action.

What the Numbers Tell Us

1. Banks pay 10–20x more than fintech for the same action

Thrivent's $300 per application vs. PayPal's $33 per user. Same "action" (a signup), wildly different payouts. Why? Customer lifetime value. A bank customer is worth thousands over a decade. A PayPal debit user is worth tens.

Takeaway for publishers: If you're going to write one article, write it about the $300 action, not the $33 one.

2. Insurance is the sleeper category

New York Life at $75/lead, USAA at $36, Simply Business at $30, Hiscox at $15, Aflac at $16. Insurance leads cluster in the $10–$75 range — consistently higher than lending or fintech. And insurance articles (life, auto, home) have massive search volume with less competition than credit cards.

3. The mortgage application is the most underpriced action in finance

BMO pays $225 per completed mortgage application. The average mortgage is $400,000+. The bank will earn $100,000+ in interest over the loan's life. They're paying $225 for a shot at that. As a publisher, you're capturing value at the very top of the funnel — before the bank has spent anything on underwriting.

4. "Free" products pay real money

Experian pays $12 for a free CreditMatch signup. WalletHub pays $50 for a premium lead that starts as a free trial. FarmTogether pays $15 for a free account signup. The pattern: free products with upsell funnels pay affiliates well because their conversion math works.

5. Funded-deal programs are a trap

Axos pays $100+ per funded account. Sounds great — until you realize 80%+ of referred users never fund. A $300 per-application payout with 100% action completion beats a $100 per-funded payout with 15% completion every time. We excluded all funded-only programs from this ranking on purpose.

The $300 Lead Economy: Why These Numbers Exist

Banks and insurers aren't being generous. They're being rational.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) in banking runs $200–$400 per customer through traditional advertising. Paying an affiliate $300 per application is cheaper than their Google Ads spend — and the affiliate only gets paid on results, not impressions.

Lifetime value (LTV) of a checking customer: $2,000–$4,000 over 10 years. Of a mortgage customer: $50,000+ in interest. Of a life insurance customer: $10,000+ in premiums.

The LTV:CAC ratio is 10:1 or better. That's why the payouts are so high — and why they'll stay high as long as banks compete for customers.

How We Got These Numbers

We read the actual program terms on CJ Affiliate (October 2026) — the legal payout schedules, not marketing pages. Every number above is the contracted affiliate payout per the specified action.

We excluded:

  • Programs paying only on funded/approved/closed deals
  • Programs with $0 action payouts
  • Programs we couldn't verify from terms

This is the first public ranking of its kind. NerdWallet, Bankrate, and Investopedia all use these same programs — they just don't tell you what they earn per click.

Which Programs We'd Actually Promote (and Why)

High payout doesn't automatically mean we'd recommend it. Here's our honest filter:

Promote with confidence: Thrivent, BMO, New York Life, USAA, Experian (free tier), WalletHub (free tier), LawDepot, TurboTax, TaxAct. Real products, real value, fair payouts.

Promote with context: Zacks Trade (good for traders, not for savers), FarmTogether (accredited investors only — most readers don't qualify), Ownwell (great if you own property, irrelevant otherwise), Sky Blue Credit (credit repair is a minefield — vet carefully).

Wouldn't promote: Anything paying only on funded deals. If the program doesn't trust its own product enough to pay per application, why should we trust it enough to recommend?

The rule: payout gets a program on the list. Product quality decides whether it stays.

What This Means for You (the Reader)

When a finance site pushes a bank account hard, now you know why: they might earn $300 when you apply. That doesn't make the recommendation bad — Thrivent and BMO are legitimate banks — but it makes you an informed consumer.

Use this information:

  • Sites pushing $300-payout products hardest have the most incentive to sell you. Read their reviews skeptically.
  • Products with low affiliate payouts (or none) that still get recommended are probably genuinely good — nobody's getting rich recommending them.
  • The best deal for you and the best payout for the site aren't always the same product. Now you can tell the difference.
